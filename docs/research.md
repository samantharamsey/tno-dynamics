## 1. Research overview {#research-overview}

Trans-Neptunian objects preserve information about the formation and evolution of the outer Solar System. This living document develops the mathematical and computational framework used to study the structures that organize their motion, with particular emphasis on the circular restricted three-body problem (CR3BP), zero-velocity geometry, periodic orbits, and transport near Neptune.

The presentation is intentionally cumulative. Each model begins with its assumptions and reference frames, proceeds through the governing equations and nondimensionalization, and ends with numerical implementation and validation. This makes the derivations auditable and creates a direct path from the mathematics to the interactive tools and research code.

## 2. Circular restricted three-body model {#cr3bp-model}

### 2.1 Bodies and assumptions {#model-assumptions}

Consider two finite masses, <span class="math-inline">\(m_1\)</span> and <span class="math-inline">\(m_2\)</span>, called the **primaries**, and a third body of mass <span class="math-inline">\(m_3\)</span>. The restricted model is obtained by requiring <span class="math-inline">\(m_3 \ll m_2 \leq m_1\)</span>, so the third body responds to the gravity of the primaries but does not measurably alter their motion. This approximation is appropriate for a spacecraft, comet, or trans-Neptunian object moving in the gravitational field of the Sun and Neptune.

The circular restricted three-body problem makes the following assumptions:

1. each primary is a point mass, or equivalently has a spherically symmetric exterior gravity field;
2. the third body is massless in the dynamical sense described above;
3. the primaries follow circular Keplerian orbits about their common barycenter;
4. the separation between the primaries and their angular speed are constant;
5. no forces other than the gravity of the two primaries are included.

The primaries therefore remain in a fixed plane, but the third body may move in all three spatial dimensions. The model is autonomous in a frame rotating with the primaries.

These assumptions are useful because they isolate the geometry generated by a single primary pair. They also define the model's limits. Planetary eccentricity, inclination, perturbations from additional planets, radiation forces, and the finite mass of the third body must be introduced separately when those effects become dynamically important.

### 2.2 Reference frames and coordinates {#reference-frames}

Let <span class="math-inline">\(\mathcal{I}=(X,Y,Z)\)</span> denote an inertial barycentric frame. Let <span class="math-inline">\(\mathcal{R}=(x,y,z)\)</span> rotate about the common <span class="math-inline">\(Z=z\)</span> axis with constant angular velocity

<div class="equation-block">\[
\boldsymbol{\omega}=n\,\hat{\mathbf{z}},
\tag{2.1}
\]</div>

where <span class="math-inline">\(n\)</span> is the mean motion of the primaries. The rotating <span class="math-inline">\(x\)</span>-axis always points from <span class="math-inline">\(m_1\)</span> toward <span class="math-inline">\(m_2\)</span>, the <span class="math-inline">\(y\)</span>-axis completes a right-handed basis in the orbital plane, and the <span class="math-inline">\(z\)</span>-axis is normal to that plane.

Let <span class="math-inline">\(a\)</span> be the constant separation between the primaries and define the mass parameter

<div class="equation-block">\[
\mu=\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2},
\qquad
1-\mu=\frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}.
\tag{2.2}
\]</div>

Because the origin is the barycenter, the dimensional primary positions in <span class="math-inline">\(\mathcal{R}\)</span> are

<div class="equation-block">\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_1=(-\mu a,0,0),
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_2=((1-\mu)a,0,0).
\tag{2.3}
\]</div>

For a third body at <span class="math-inline">\(\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}=(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y},\widetilde{z})\)</span>, define the displacement vectors from the primaries to the third body as

<div class="equation-block">\[
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_1
=\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_1,
\qquad
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_2
=\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_2,
\tag{2.4}
\]</div>

with magnitudes

<div class="equation-block">\[
\widetilde{\rho}_1
=\sqrt{(\widetilde{x}+\mu a)^2+\widetilde{y}^2+\widetilde{z}^2},
\qquad
\widetilde{\rho}_2
=\sqrt{(\widetilde{x}-(1-\mu)a)^2+\widetilde{y}^2+\widetilde{z}^2}.
\tag{2.5}
\]</div>

A tilde marks a dimensional quantity. Untilded variables introduced later are nondimensional.

## 3. From Newton's law to the rotating equations {#governing-equations}

### 3.1 Inertial equation of motion {#inertial-equation}

Newton's second law in the inertial frame gives

<div class="equation-block">\[
m_3\,{}^{\mathcal I}\!\ddot{\widetilde{\mathbf r}}
=-\frac{Gm_1m_3}{\widetilde{\rho}_1^3}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_1
-\frac{Gm_2m_3}{\widetilde{\rho}_2^3}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_2.
\tag{3.1}
\]</div>

The factor <span class="math-inline">\(m_3\)</span> cancels. This is the mathematical consequence of the restricted approximation: the acceleration of the third body depends on the primaries and its position, but not on its own mass.

### 3.2 Transport theorem {#transport-theorem}

For any vector <span class="math-inline">\(\mathbf{q}\)</span>, derivatives observed in the inertial and rotating frames are related by

<div class="equation-block">\[
\left(\frac{d\mathbf q}{dt}\right)_{\!\mathcal I}
=
\left(\frac{d\mathbf q}{dt}\right)_{\!\mathcal R}
+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf q.
\tag{3.2}
\]</div>

Applying the theorem twice to position produces the acceleration identity

<div class="equation-block">\[
{}^{\mathcal I}\!\ddot{\widetilde{\mathbf r}}
=
{}^{\mathcal R}\!\ddot{\widetilde{\mathbf r}}
+2\boldsymbol{\omega}\times{}^{\mathcal R}\!\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf r}}
+\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times\widetilde{\mathbf r}
+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times
(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\widetilde{\mathbf r}).
\tag{3.3}
\]</div>

The four terms on the right are the rotating-frame acceleration, the Coriolis acceleration, the Euler acceleration, and the centrifugal acceleration. Circular motion makes <span class="math-inline">\(n\)</span> constant, so <span class="math-inline">\(\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=0\)</span>. Expanding the remaining cross products gives

<div class="equation-block">\[
{}^{\mathcal I}\!\ddot{\widetilde{\mathbf r}}
=
(\ddot{\widetilde{x}}-2n\dot{\widetilde{y}}-n^2\widetilde{x})\hat{\mathbf x}
+(\ddot{\widetilde{y}}+2n\dot{\widetilde{x}}-n^2\widetilde{y})\hat{\mathbf y}
+\ddot{\widetilde{z}}\hat{\mathbf z}.
\tag{3.4}
\]</div>

Substitution into Eq. (3.1) yields the dimensional rotating-frame equations

<div class="equation-block">\[
\begin{aligned}
\ddot{\widetilde{x}}-2n\dot{\widetilde{y}}-n^2\widetilde{x}
&=-Gm_1\frac{\widetilde{x}+\mu a}{\widetilde{\rho}_1^3}
-Gm_2\frac{\widetilde{x}-(1-\mu)a}{\widetilde{\rho}_2^3},\\
\ddot{\widetilde{y}}+2n\dot{\widetilde{x}}-n^2\widetilde{y}
&=-Gm_1\frac{\widetilde{y}}{\widetilde{\rho}_1^3}
-Gm_2\frac{\widetilde{y}}{\widetilde{\rho}_2^3},\\
\ddot{\widetilde{z}}
&=-Gm_1\frac{\widetilde{z}}{\widetilde{\rho}_1^3}
-Gm_2\frac{\widetilde{z}}{\widetilde{\rho}_2^3}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.5}
\]</div>

The Coriolis terms couple in-plane position and velocity. The centrifugal term has no <span class="math-inline">\(z\)</span>-component because the frame rotates about the <span class="math-inline">\(z\)</span>-axis.

### 3.3 Characteristic quantities {#characteristic-quantities}

The natural scales of the primary system are

<div class="equation-block">\[
l^*=a,
\qquad
m^*=m_1+m_2,
\qquad
t^*=\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{G(m_1+m_2)}}=\frac{1}{n}.
\tag{3.6}
\]</div>

Kepler's third law gives <span class="math-inline">\(n^2a^3=G(m_1+m_2)\)</span>. The characteristic time <span class="math-inline">\(t^*=1/n\)</span> is the time associated with one radian of the primaries' motion; the full dimensional orbital period is <span class="math-inline">\(2\pi t^*\)</span>.

Define nondimensional variables by

<div class="equation-block">\[
\mathbf r=\frac{\widetilde{\mathbf r}}{a},
\qquad
\tau=nt,
\qquad
\mathbf v=\frac{\widetilde{\mathbf v}}{an}.
\tag{3.7}
\]</div>

The conversion rules that will be used throughout the computational work are therefore

<div class="equation-block">\[
\widetilde{\mathbf r}=a\mathbf r,
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf v}=an\mathbf v,
\qquad
\widetilde{t}=\frac{\tau}{n}.
\tag{3.8}
\]</div>

This normalization sets the primary separation, total mass, gravitational parameter, and mean motion to unity. It also makes results transferable between systems with different physical scales but the same mass parameter <span class="math-inline">\(\mu\)</span>.

### 3.4 Nondimensional equations {#nondimensional-equations}

In normalized coordinates the primaries are fixed at

<div class="equation-block">\[
\mathbf r_1=(-\mu,0,0),
\qquad
\mathbf r_2=(1-\mu,0,0),
\tag{3.9}
\]</div>

and the distances to the third body are

<div class="equation-block">\[
r_1=\sqrt{(x+\mu)^2+y^2+z^2},
\qquad
r_2=\sqrt{(x-1+\mu)^2+y^2+z^2}.
\tag{3.10}
\]</div>

Dots now denote differentiation with respect to nondimensional time <span class="math-inline">\(\tau\)</span>. The equations of motion become

<div class="equation-block">\[
\begin{aligned}
\ddot{x}-2\dot{y}
&=x-(1-\mu)\frac{x+\mu}{r_1^3}
-\mu\frac{x-1+\mu}{r_2^3},\\
\ddot{y}+2\dot{x}
&=y-(1-\mu)\frac{y}{r_1^3}
-\mu\frac{y}{r_2^3},\\
\ddot{z}
&=-(1-\mu)\frac{z}{r_1^3}
-\mu\frac{z}{r_2^3}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.11}
\]</div>

## 4. Effective potential and the Jacobi integral {#jacobi-geometry}

### 4.1 Effective potential {#effective-potential}

Define the rotating-frame effective potential

<div class="equation-block">\[
\Omega(x,y,z)
=\frac{1}{2}(x^2+y^2)
+\frac{1-\mu}{r_1}
+\frac{\mu}{r_2}.
\tag{4.1}
\]</div>

The first term is the centrifugal contribution and the remaining terms are gravitational. Equation (3.11) can be written compactly as

<div class="equation-block">\[
\ddot{x}-2\dot{y}=\Omega_x,
\qquad
\ddot{y}+2\dot{x}=\Omega_y,
\qquad
\ddot{z}=\Omega_z,
\tag{4.2}
\]</div>

where subscripts denote partial derivatives.

### 4.2 Derivation of the integral {#jacobi-derivation}

Multiply the three equations in Eq. (4.2) by <span class="math-inline">\(\dot{x}\)</span>, <span class="math-inline">\(\dot{y}\)</span>, and <span class="math-inline">\(\dot{z}\)</span>, respectively, and add them. The two Coriolis terms cancel because they do no work:

<div class="equation-block">\[
\dot{x}\ddot{x}+\dot{y}\ddot{y}+\dot{z}\ddot{z}
=\Omega_x\dot{x}+\Omega_y\dot{y}+\Omega_z\dot{z}.
\tag{4.3}
\]</div>

Both sides are total derivatives,

<div class="equation-block">\[
\frac{d}{d\tau}
\left[\frac{1}{2}(\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2+\dot{z}^2)\right]
=\frac{d\Omega}{d\tau}.
\tag{4.4}
\]</div>

so integration gives the Jacobi constant

<div class="equation-block">\[
C=2\Omega(x,y,z)
-\left(\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2+\dot{z}^2\right).
\tag{4.5}
\]</div>

The CR3BP is autonomous in the rotating frame, and <span class="math-inline">\(C\)</span> is its conserved first integral. It is related to the rotating-frame Hamiltonian by <span class="math-inline">\(C=-2H\)</span>; increasing <span class="math-inline">\(C\)</span> therefore corresponds to decreasing energy.

### 4.3 Accessible and forbidden regions {#zero-velocity-regions}

Solving Eq. (4.5) for speed gives

<div class="equation-block">\[
v^2=\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2+\dot{z}^2=2\Omega-C.
\tag{4.6}
\]</div>

A real trajectory requires <span class="math-inline">\(v^2\geq0\)</span>. Configuration-space points satisfying <span class="math-inline">\(2\Omega<C\)</span> are therefore forbidden at that Jacobi constant. The boundary

<div class="equation-block">\[
2\Omega(x,y,z)=C
\tag{4.7}
\]</div>

is the zero-velocity surface. It constrains where a trajectory may travel without prescribing the path or the time required to move between accessible regions.

As <span class="math-inline">\(C\)</span> decreases, necks open at the collinear equilibrium points. These openings change the connectivity of the accessible region and create geometric gateways between neighborhoods of the primaries and the exterior domain. ## 5. Equilibrium points {#equilibrium-points}

### 5.1 Equilibrium condition {#equilibrium-condition}

An equilibrium point is stationary in the rotating frame, so its velocity and acceleration are both zero. Equation (4.2) then requires

<div class="equation-block">\[
\nabla\Omega(x,y,z)=\mathbf 0.
\tag{5.1}
\]</div>

The <span class="math-inline">\(z\)</span>-equation implies (z=0), so all five classical equilibrium points lie in the orbital plane. Three are collinear with the primaries and two form equilateral triangles with them.

### 5.2 Triangular points {#triangular-points}

For an equilibrium with <span class="math-inline">\(y\neq0\)</span>, the <span class="math-inline">\(x\)</span>- and <span class="math-inline">\(y\)</span>-conditions require equal unit distances from the third body to both primaries: <span class="math-inline">\(r_1=r_2=1\)</span>. The two solutions are

<div class="equation-block">\[
L_4=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu,\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},0\right),
\qquad
L_5=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu,-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},0\right).
\tag{5.2}
\]</div>

Their existence does not guarantee linear stability. In the CR3BP, the triangular points are linearly stable only when the mass parameter is below the Routh value

<div class="equation-block">\[
\mu_R=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{23}{27}}\right)\approx0.03852.
\tag{5.3}
\]</div>

### 5.3 Collinear points and root finding {#collinear-points}

For the collinear points, (y=z=0), and the equilibrium condition reduces to a scalar equation containing absolute values. It is convenient to solve for the positive distance <span class="math-inline">\(\gamma_i\)</span> from the nearest primary. Define

<div class="equation-block">\[
x_{L_1}=1-\mu-\gamma_1,
\qquad
x_{L_2}=1-\mu+\gamma_2,
\qquad
x_{L_3}=-\mu-\gamma_3.
\tag{5.4}
\]</div>

Substitution into Eq. (5.1) gives one fifth-degree polynomial for each region:

<div class="equation-block">\[
\begin{aligned}
f_1(\gamma_1)
&=\gamma_1^5-(3-\mu)\gamma_1^4+(3-2\mu)\gamma_1^3
-\mu\gamma_1^2+2\mu\gamma_1-\mu,\\
f_2(\gamma_2)
&=\gamma_2^5+(3-\mu)\gamma_2^4+(3-2\mu)\gamma_2^3
-\mu\gamma_2^2-2\mu\gamma_2-\mu,\\
f_3(\gamma_3)
&=\gamma_3^5+(2+\mu)\gamma_3^4+(1+2\mu)\gamma_3^3
-(1-\mu)\gamma_3^2-2(1-\mu)\gamma_3-(1-\mu).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.5}
\]</div>

The roots are computed iteratively with Newton's method,

<div class="equation-block">\[
\gamma_i^{(k+1)}
=\gamma_i^{(k)}
-\frac{f_i(\gamma_i^{(k)})}{f_i'(\gamma_i^{(k)})}.
\tag{5.6}
\]</div>

Iteration stops only after both the update and the polynomial residual are sufficiently small. Bracketing or safeguarded Newton methods are preferable when an initial guess may cross into the wrong spatial region.

Using the mass values currently adopted by the interactive model gives

<div class="equation-block">\[
\mu_{SN}=5.1485534\times10^{-5}.
\tag{5.7}
\]</div>

The corresponding nondimensional equilibrium locations and critical Jacobi constants are:

## 6. Numerical propagation and verification {#numerical-methods}

### 6.1 First-order state model {#state-model}

Numerical integrators operate on a first-order system. Define

<div class="equation-block">\[
\mathbf X=
\begin{bmatrix}
x&y&z&\dot{x}&\dot{y}&\dot{z}
\end{bmatrix}^{\!T}.
\tag{6.1}
\]</div>

Then

<div class="equation-block">\[
\dot{\mathbf X}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{x}\\
\dot{y}\\
\dot{z}\\
2\dot{y}+\Omega_x\\
-2\dot{x}+\Omega_y\\
\Omega_z
\end{bmatrix}.
\tag{6.2}
\]</div>

This expression is the common interface between the analytical model and an adaptive numerical integrator.

### 6.2 Dimensional reconstruction {#dimensional-reconstruction}

Nondimensional results are converted back to physical units with Eq. (3.8). For a primary system with separation <span class="math-inline">\(a\)</span>, total gravitational parameter <span class="math-inline">\(G(m_1+m_2)\)</span>, and state <span class="math-inline">\((\mathbf r,\mathbf v)\)</span>,

<div class="equation-block">\[
\widetilde{\mathbf r}=a\mathbf r,
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf v}=\sqrt{\frac{G(m_1+m_2)}{a}}\,\mathbf v.
\tag{6.3}
\]</div>

Reporting both normalized and dimensional values prevents scale conversions from becoming hidden implementation details.

### 6.3 Jacobi conservation as a diagnostic {#jacobi-diagnostic}

For an unforced CR3BP trajectory, the computed Jacobi constant should remain equal to its initial value. Define the drift

<div class="equation-block">\[
\Delta C(\tau)=C(\mathbf X(\tau))-C(\mathbf X(0)).
\tag{6.4}
\]</div>

The maximum and root-mean-square values of <span class="math-inline">\(\Delta C\)</span> are recorded for every propagation. A small Jacobi drift is necessary but not sufficient for an accurate trajectory: long integrations near separatrices can remain highly sensitive to small changes in tolerances, initial conditions, and arithmetic precision.

A defensible convergence study therefore repeats the propagation with progressively tighter relative and absolute tolerances and compares:

- the complete state history over a shared time interval;
- event times such as crossings of a chosen surface;
- the maximum Jacobi drift;
- the result under a reduced maximum step size; and
- agreement between independent integration methods when practical.

The tightest tolerance is not automatically the best scientific choice. A tolerance is accepted when the quantities used in the analysis have converged while the computational cost remains reasonable.

### 6.4 Benchmark gateway trajectory {#benchmark-trajectory}

An Earth-Moon trajectory provides a useful benchmark because the opening near <span class="math-inline">\(L_1\)</span> is easy to interpret. For

<div class="equation-block">\[
\mu=0.012153659,
\qquad
\mathbf r_0=(-0.270,-0.420,0),
\qquad
\mathbf v_0=(0.300,-1.000,0),
\tag{6.5}
\]</div>

Eq. (4.5) gives <span class="math-inline">\(C_0=3.18647488\)</span>. Since this value is slightly below the Earth-Moon <span class="math-inline">\(L_1\)</span> critical value, the zero-velocity curve contains a narrow gateway connecting the two primary neighborhoods while the outer gateways remain closed.

With <span class="math-inline">\(a=384{,}400\ \mathrm{km}\)</span> and <span class="math-inline">\(t^*=375{,}190.3\ \mathrm{s}\)</span>, the same initial state is

<div class="equation-block">\[
\widetilde{\mathbf r}_0
=(-103{,}788,-161{,}448,0)\ \mathrm{km},
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf v}_0
=(0.30736,-1.02455,0)\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.
\tag{6.6}
\]</div>

Long integrations of this state can pass through the narrow <span class="math-inline">\(L_1\)</span> gateway. The geometry explains where passage is possible, while the trajectory determines whether and when it occurs. Because the motion near the gateway is sensitive, a reported transfer time must be accompanied by the integrator, tolerances, event definition, and a convergence study. This turns the former classroom example into a reproducible numerical benchmark.

### 6.5 Verification checklist {#verification-checklist}

Before a trajectory or surface is used in the research, the implementation is checked against the following conditions:

1. the primary positions satisfy the barycenter relation;
2. the computed equilibrium points make <span class="math-inline">\(\lVert\nabla\Omega\rVert\)</span> numerically zero;
3. the Jacobi constant at each equilibrium matches the reported critical value;
4. the zero-velocity boundary satisfies <span class="math-inline">\(2\Omega-C=0\)</span> to the extraction tolerance;
5. dimensional and nondimensional states round-trip through the unit conversion;
6. Jacobi drift and state histories converge as integration tolerances are tightened; and
7. coordinate orientation and sign conventions agree across equations, code, and figures.

## 7. Role in trans-Neptunian dynamics {#tno-connection}

For the Sun-Neptune pair, the CR3BP provides a geometric baseline for studying motion near Neptune's orbit. Its equilibria, periodic orbits, and invariant manifolds organize pathways through phase space and supply a language for discussing temporary capture, resonance transitions, and transport between dynamically distinct regions.

The model is not a complete representation of the outer Solar System. Neptune's eccentricity, its inclination relative to a chosen reference plane, the gravity of the other giant planets, and resonant forcing can all modify the motion. The research strategy is therefore hierarchical: first identify mechanisms in the autonomous Sun-Neptune CR3BP, then test which structures persist as additional physical effects are introduced.

Planned extensions of this chapter include:

- linearization and stability near the five equilibrium points;
- differential correction and continuation of periodic-orbit families;
- state-transition matrices and monodromy analysis;
- stable and unstable invariant manifolds;
- surface-of-section and resonance diagnostics;
- comparison with higher-fidelity ephemeris models; and
- direct links from each derivation to reproducible code, figures, and data.
